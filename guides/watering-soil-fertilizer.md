# Watering, Soil, Mulch & Fertilizer

Cherry tomatoes are productive plants, but many common problems come from inconsistent water or unnecessary fertilizer rather than a lack of products.

## 1. Start with drainage and a soil test

Rutgers recommends growing tomatoes in well-drained soil and beginning with a soil test. A soil test tells you the soil pH and nutrient status and gives amendment recommendations.

For tomatoes, Rutgers identifies a soil pH of about **6.0–6.5** as optimal in its home tomato guide.

### Why this matters

Do not automatically add lime, calcium, phosphorus, or a strong fertilizer just because the plant looks stressed. Similar symptoms can have different causes, and excess fertilizer can create new problems.

## 2. Water deeply and consistently

For in-ground tomatoes, Rutgers recommends at least about **1 inch of water each week**, including rainfall.

The goal is not a rigid timer. The goal is a root zone that does not repeatedly swing from very dry to saturated.

Good practice:

- water slowly enough that water soaks into the root zone;
- favor deeper watering over frequent shallow sprinkling;
- irrigate the soil, not the foliage, when practical;
- water in the morning so accidental leaf wetness can dry sooner; and
- check soil after unusually hot, windy, or dry weather.

## 3. Why even moisture matters

Rutgers connects irregular soil moisture with two important tomato problems:

### Fruit cracking

A heavy rain or large irrigation after a dry period can cause fruit to expand rapidly and crack. More even moisture lowers this risk.

### Blossom-end rot

Blossom-end rot appears as a dark, sunken, leathery area at the blossom end of the fruit. Rutgers explains that it is a physiological disorder related to calcium reaching the fruit; disrupted root water uptake is a common trigger.

That means the first response should be to correct moisture and root-zone problems, not automatically to spray calcium on the plant.

## 4. Mulch

Rutgers recommends mulch as a useful tomato-management tool.

Benefits include:

- reducing evaporation;
- reducing moisture swings;
- suppressing weeds;
- limiting soil splash onto lower foliage and fruit; and
- helping moderate soil temperature.

Rutgers suggests organic materials such as straw, salt hay, or dried grass clippings. If grass clippings came from a lawn treated with herbicide, follow Rutgers' precautions before using them around vegetables.

Apply organic mulch after the soil has warmed rather than insulating cold spring soil too early.

## 5. Fertilizer: use enough, not as much as possible

High nitrogen can make tomatoes produce excessive foliage while flowering and fruiting poorly.

Rutgers' tomato guide gives a side-dress program for garden-grown tomatoes after fruit begins developing and again around first harvest. Use its rate or, preferably, the recommendation from your soil test.

### Avoid these common mistakes

- Feeding every time the plant looks slightly pale without identifying the cause.
- Using a high-nitrogen lawn fertilizer around tomatoes.
- Adding lime every year without knowing the pH.
- Assuming blossom-end rot proves the soil lacks calcium.
- Applying concentrated fertilizer to dry roots.

## 6. If the plant is in a container

Container plants behave differently because their root volume is restricted and nutrients can leach out with frequent watering. See [Container Growing](container-growing.md) for the Rutgers container-specific recommendations.
