# Pests, Diseases & Disorders — New Jersey Cherry Tomatoes

The safest useful rule is: **identify the problem before treating it**. Rutgers emphasizes monitoring and diagnosis because insect damage, fungal disease, nutrient stress, and environmental disorders can look similar.

## A simple inspection routine

Rutgers recommends scouting the vegetable garden at least **twice weekly**.

Check:

- newest leaves and growing tips;
- upper and lower leaf surfaces;
- stems and branch junctions;
- flowers and fruit;
- lower foliage near the soil; and
- the ground beneath the plant for dropped leaves, frass, or damaged fruit.

## Common fungal diseases in New Jersey home tomatoes

Rutgers identifies several important fungal diseases, including:

- **Early blight**
- **Septoria leaf spot**
- **Anthracnose fruit rot**
- **Late blight**
- **Buckeye rot**

Do not diagnose only from one brown spot. Compare the pattern, which plant parts are affected, weather conditions, and progression over time.

## Disease prevention that matters most

Rutgers recommends cultural prevention before relying on fungicides:

1. Rotate tomatoes away from locations where tomatoes, peppers, eggplants, or potatoes recently grew.
2. Give plants full sun and good air movement.
3. Keep plants supported rather than sprawling on wet soil.
4. Water in the morning when possible.
5. Direct irrigation toward soil rather than regularly wetting leaves.
6. Mulch to reduce soil splash onto foliage and fruit.
7. Remove infected tomato debris at the end of the season.

Rutgers' tomato fungal-disease guide considers a **three-year rotation** important for reducing soil-borne fungal pressure when space allows.

## Hornworms

Tomato and tobacco hornworms occur in New Jersey and can defoliate tomato plants quickly.

### Signs

- missing leaves or bare stems;
- large chewing damage;
- damage starting high on the plant; and
- dark green-black frass pellets below feeding areas.

### What to do

For a small home garden, inspect carefully and hand-pick larvae. Rutgers notes that hornworms are easier to locate around dawn/dusk and may be on leaf undersides.

If a hornworm has multiple small white parasitoid cocoons on its body, Rutgers recommends leaving it in place because beneficial braconid wasps are developing from it and can help suppress other hornworms.

## Aphids, whiteflies, mites, and stink bugs

These can also occur on home vegetables. Before treating:

- confirm the pest is actually present;
- check undersides of leaves;
- distinguish pests from beneficial insects; and
- consider nonchemical controls first when practical.

Rutgers' current home-garden insect guide lists controls by pest and should be checked before any pesticide use because registrations and labels can change.

## Blossom-end rot: not an infectious disease

### Appearance

A dark, sunken, leathery patch develops at the blossom end of a tomato.

### Cause

Rutgers describes blossom-end rot as a physiological disorder associated with insufficient calcium reaching rapidly developing fruit, often because root water uptake is disrupted by dry soil, saturated soil, or damaged roots.

### Response

- keep soil moisture more consistent;
- make sure drainage is adequate;
- avoid damaging roots with deep cultivation;
- use a soil test to determine whether pH/lime correction is actually needed; and
- do not assume a calcium spray is the solution.

## Fruit cracking

Cracking often follows a moisture swing, such as a heavy rain after dry conditions. Improve watering consistency and use mulch to reduce fluctuations.

## Sunscald

A pale white/yellow area can form on fruit that becomes overexposed to strong sun. Avoid removing too much healthy foliage around developing fruit.

## Lots of leaves, few fruits

Rutgers lists possible causes including:

- excessive nitrogen;
- very high temperatures;
- very low temperatures; and
- heavy rainfall conditions that interfere with flower set.

The controllable first step is to review fertilizer use instead of adding more.

## When should you use a pesticide?

Only after identifying the pest or disease and checking the current recommendation. If a pesticide is needed:

- use a product labeled for the crop and problem;
- follow the current label exactly;
- observe harvest intervals and application restrictions; and
- re-check Rutgers guidance because approved products can change.
