# New Jersey Seasonal Calendar for Cherry Tomatoes

New Jersey has meaningful climate differences from the warmer south/coast to the colder northwest, so this calendar uses **frost status and plant stage** rather than pretending one date works everywhere.

Rutgers notes that the last frost for warm-season crops can range roughly from **April 20 in extreme southern New Jersey to June 1 in the colder northwest**. Tomatoes should go outside only after frost danger has passed.

## January–February: plan

- Choose a sunny location with good drainage.
- Rotate away from places where tomatoes, peppers, eggplants, or potatoes recently grew when possible.
- Order seed if starting your own plants.
- Favor varieties with disease resistance where practical.
- Consider a soil test before adding lime or fertilizer.

## March–April: start seed indoors

Rutgers recommends starting tomato seed about **6–8 weeks before the expected outdoor transplant date**.

For seedlings:

- use clean/sterile growing medium;
- provide strong light so plants do not become leggy;
- keep them warm; and
- begin hardening them gradually before transplanting outdoors.

Do not use a fixed statewide transplant date. Your local last-frost date matters.

## Late April–June: transplant after frost danger

Rutgers classifies tomatoes as a warm-season crop and warns that frost can kill them.

At planting:

- choose a site receiving at least 8 hours of direct sun;
- plant into well-drained soil;
- bury part of the stem so additional roots can develop;
- install cages/stakes early;
- water after transplanting; and
- give plants enough spacing for air movement.

Rutgers' general home-garden planting table places tomato transplants in **May and June**, but local frost conditions should override a calendar shortcut.

## Late spring–early summer: establish the plant

Once plants are growing:

- keep soil moisture steady;
- water slowly and deeply;
- control weeds;
- add mulch after the soil has warmed;
- guide stems into cages or tie to stakes gently; and
- begin checking for insects and disease at least twice weekly.

Avoid pushing the plant with excessive nitrogen.

## Summer: flowering, fruiting, and harvest

This is the main care period.

### Water

Rutgers recommends at least about 1 inch per week for in-ground tomatoes, including rainfall. Hot weather, sandy soil, wind, and containers can increase water demand.

### Support

Keep vines supported. Vigorous indeterminate cherry tomatoes can grow more than 6 feet tall under good conditions.

### Feeding

For garden-grown plants, Rutgers recommends side-dressing when developing fruits are about 1 inch in diameter and again around first harvest, using the rate in its tomato guide or a soil-test-based program.

### Disease prevention

Warm, humid, rainy New Jersey weather can favor fungal disease. Improve air movement, avoid unnecessary leaf wetting, irrigate in the morning when possible, mulch against soil splash, and remove badly affected material.

### Pest checks

Look especially for:

- hornworms and their dark frass;
- aphids;
- stink bugs/plant bugs;
- spider mites;
- whiteflies; and
- other chewing or sap-feeding pests.

Use identification first, control second.

## September: keep harvesting; protect plant health

Healthy indeterminate cherry tomatoes can continue producing until frost.

Priorities:

- keep picking ripe fruit;
- continue consistent watering if rainfall is insufficient;
- inspect for late-season disease and pests;
- avoid unnecessary heavy nitrogen feeding;
- keep foliage and fruit off the soil; and
- watch the forecast as nights become cooler.

If foliage becomes heavily diseased, sanitation and removal of infected material become increasingly important.

## October–first frost: finish the season

Tomatoes are very frost-sensitive. When frost ends the plant:

- harvest usable fruit beforehand when practical;
- remove tomato plant debris from the garden;
- do not leave diseased vines lying in the bed; and
- plan to rotate next year's tomatoes away from the same Solanaceae bed when space permits.

Rutgers' fungal-disease guidance recommends removing and discarding infected tomato refuse rather than relying on an inadequately managed home compost pile to destroy pathogens.

## Winter: reset for next year

- Record which varieties performed best.
- Note disease or pest problems.
- Plan rotation.
- Clean reusable support equipment before the next crop.
- Revisit soil-test recommendations rather than applying fertilizer or lime automatically.

## New Jersey cherry varieties from Rutgers trials

Rutgers lists these cherry types among varieties that have performed well in New Jersey:

- Matt's Wild Cherry
- Sun Gold
- Sugar Snack
- Sun Sugar
- Sweet 100
