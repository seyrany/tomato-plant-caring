# Support & Pruning Cherry Tomatoes

Cherry tomato vines can become large and heavy, especially indeterminate varieties. The goal of support is to keep the plant upright, fruit off the soil, and foliage better exposed to air and light without removing so much foliage that fruit is exposed to sunscald.

## Cages

Cages are a simple choice for home gardens.

Rutgers notes that cages:

- keep fruit off the ground;
- reduce contact with some soil-borne rots; and
- preserve more foliage around fruit than aggressive staking/pruning systems.

Use a sturdy cage and anchor it well. As stems grow through the openings, gently guide them back inside when practical.

## Stakes

Rutgers describes stakes about **6 feet long** for traditional tomato staking. Vigorous indeterminate cherry tomatoes may need similarly tall or taller support.

Tie stems with soft material rather than wire or tight plastic. Rutgers describes a figure-eight tie that keeps the stem from rubbing directly against the stake.

## Stake-and-weave

For several plants in a row, Rutgers' **FS1102** describes the stake-and-weave method. It uses stakes and horizontal twine to support the plants as they grow.

This can be useful when you have several cherry tomato plants and want easier harvest access than a dense cage row.

## What is a sucker?

A sucker is the side shoot that develops in the angle between a leaf stem and the main stem.

## Should you remove suckers?

Not always.

Rutgers explains that a traditional staked tomato can be trained to one or two main stems by removing suckers while they are small. This has tradeoffs:

**Possible advantages**

- somewhat earlier fruit;
- sometimes larger individual fruit; and
- a narrower plant that is easier to support.

**Possible disadvantages**

- lower total yield per plant; and
- more fruit exposure, increasing sunscald risk.

For a home-grown cherry tomato, especially one in a cage, you do **not** need to remove every sucker. Light management is often enough.

## A conservative pruning approach

For a healthy caged cherry tomato:

1. Remove leaves touching the soil when practical.
2. Remove clearly diseased, dead, or badly damaged leaves.
3. Thin only enough to improve access or air movement if the plant becomes extremely dense.
4. Avoid stripping healthy mature leaves from around fruit.
5. If using a strict one- or two-stem stake system, remove suckers when they are small rather than tearing out large branches later.

## Why not remove too many leaves?

Rutgers warns that sunscald can appear as a white or yellowish patch on fruit directly exposed to intense sun. Healthy foliage shades fruit and helps prevent this problem.

## Sanitation

If pruning diseased material:

- avoid moving from a clearly diseased plant to a healthy one with contaminated hands/tools when practical;
- remove diseased debris from the garden; and
- do not leave infected tomato refuse under the plant.

For reusable stakes, Rutgers FS1102 discusses disinfecting them before reuse to reduce carryover of disease organisms.
