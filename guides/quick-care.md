# Quick Cherry Tomato Care Guide

This page is the everyday checklist for a cherry tomato plant in a New Jersey home garden.

## Light

Choose the sunniest practical location. Rutgers recommends **at least 8 hours of direct sun per day** for fruiting vegetables such as tomatoes.

## Water

For tomatoes growing in the ground, Rutgers recommends at least about **1 inch of water per week**, counting rainfall and irrigation. Water slowly and deeply rather than giving frequent shallow splashes.

The most important habit is **consistent soil moisture**. A dry period followed by a large amount of water can contribute to fruit cracking, and uneven moisture is associated with blossom-end rot.

When possible:

- water in the morning;
- direct water to the soil instead of routinely wetting leaves;
- use a soaker hose or drip-style irrigation if available; and
- mulch to slow moisture loss and reduce soil splash.

## Soil

Tomatoes prefer well-drained soil. Rutgers recommends beginning with a soil test because fertilizer and lime needs depend on the existing soil.

Rutgers gives an optimum tomato soil pH range of approximately **6.0–6.5** in its home tomato guide. Avoid adding lime simply because tomatoes need calcium; first determine whether the soil actually needs it.

## Fertilizer

Do not feed heavily with nitrogen. Too much nitrogen can produce a large green plant with relatively little fruit.

For garden-grown tomatoes, Rutgers recommends side-dressing after fruit begins to develop and again around first harvest, using the rates in its tomato guide or a soil-test-based recommendation. If you use a packaged fertilizer, follow its label rather than improvising a stronger dose.

## Mulch

After the soil has warmed in late spring, an organic mulch can help:

- keep moisture more even;
- reduce weeds;
- reduce soil splashing onto lower leaves and fruit; and
- moderate soil temperature.

Rutgers suggests materials such as straw and dried grass clippings, with precautions about herbicide-treated lawn clippings.

## Support

Most vigorous cherry tomatoes benefit from a cage, stake, or trellis. Supporting the plant keeps fruit off the ground, improves access and can help foliage dry faster.

Install support early so you do not damage a large root system later.

## Pruning

Do **not** treat aggressive pruning as mandatory.

If you stake an indeterminate plant, Rutgers describes a one- or two-stem system in which small suckers are removed while still short. However, Rutgers also notes tradeoffs: staking/pruning can produce somewhat earlier or larger fruit but can reduce total yield and increase sunscald risk.

For a caged cherry tomato, light cleanup and removal of damaged or diseased foliage is usually more important than stripping the plant heavily.

## Daily / weekly routine

### Every day or two in hot weather

- Check soil moisture, especially in containers.
- Pick ripe or nearly ripe fruit.
- Look for sudden wilting or broken branches.

### At least twice each week

Rutgers recommends regular pest scouting. Inspect:

- upper and lower leaf surfaces;
- new growth;
- flowers and fruit;
- lower leaves near the soil; and
- stems for chewing damage or hornworm frass.

## When fruit cracks

Cracking commonly follows a large moisture change, such as heavy rain after dry soil. Aim for more even watering rather than trying to “repair” cracked fruit.

## When the fruit has a black, leathery bottom

This is often **blossom-end rot**, a physiological disorder rather than an infectious disease. Rutgers links it to inadequate calcium reaching developing fruit, commonly because water uptake is disrupted. Keep soil moisture even and avoid damaging roots. A soil test can determine whether pH or calcium correction is actually needed.

## When leaves develop spots

Do not immediately spray. First identify the problem. Several fungal diseases occur on New Jersey tomatoes, including early blight, Septoria leaf spot, anthracnose and late blight.

Good prevention includes:

- crop rotation;
- full sun and air movement;
- keeping foliage dry when practical;
- morning irrigation;
- mulching to reduce soil splash; and
- removing infected tomato debris at the end of the season.
